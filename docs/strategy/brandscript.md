## North-Star Vision

**More of the world the GM imagined becomes part of the world the players experience.**

Every element of this BrandScript should support this outcome without implying that
MasterMind creates the story, guarantees player investment, or replaces the GM's judgment.

---

## 1. Character: The Hero

### Who are they?

A GM beginning their next ambitious campaign or substantial adaptation.

- They want to create a world that feels alive — one filled with layered characters,
  meaningful relationships, evolving circumstances, and player choices that produce
  coherent consequences.
- They have learned from previous campaigns how difficult it is to sustain that depth
  when the world is spread across separate pages, files, and folders.

### What do they want?

To create a campaign their players become invested in because the world feels alive and
their choices genuinely matter.

They want:

- Characters whose motivations make sense within their histories, relationships, and
  surroundings.
- A world whose factions, places, and events affect one another coherently.
- Player choices that produce consequences beyond the immediate moment.
- Credible possibilities they can draw upon when players do something unexpected.
- More of their imagination and preparation to produce value at the table.
- To give people they care about a meaningful shared experience.

### 3 truths about this hero

1. **They volunteered for the hardest job at the table.** This deserves to be said out
   loud, early, because almost nobody says it to them. Groups struggle to find a GM at
   all — it's time-consuming, intimidating, and it asks you to be publicly, continuously
   vulnerable while projecting confidence. The person who raises their hand is doing
   something the other four aren't. Everything else in this document sits on top of that
   fact.
2. **They're ambitious, not overwhelmed.** They don't want a managed campaign. They want
   a world where the fisherman knows the gang three streets over, where a decision in
   session two comes back in session nine, where the players feel like the place existed
   before they arrived.
3. **Their imagination is not the bottleneck. Never imply otherwise.** What runs out
   isn't ideas — it's the ability to hold a hundred connected things in mind at once,
   which no one can do.

**Who this is not (internal only):** the GM running straight from the book, the GM who's
happy with minimal prep, the GM who genuinely holds it all. We don't need them.

## 2. Problem

### Villain: "The Page-by-Page Trap"

**Your world is connected. Your notes show it one page at a time.**

- A campaign is a connected web of characters, places, motives, histories, events, and
  consequences.
- Pages, files, and folders reveal that world one piece at a time.
- The GM's notes are not defective. The limitation is that story elements capable of
  affecting one another are rarely visible together.

### External problem

When interconnected campaign elements live on separate pages, their relationships and
implications can become easier to overlook.

This can affect the GM at several moments:

- **While worldbuilding:** Looking at one element at a time can make missing connective
  tissue, unexplored relationships, and useful questions harder to notice.
- **While developing characters and factions:** A person's history, environment,
  relationships, and motives may live in different places, making the full context harder
  to consider together.
- **While preparing:** The GM starts from where the players left off and what they intend
  or seem likely to pursue. When campaign material is spread across separate notes,
  preparation can be shaped by what comes to mind first or what the GM remembers to look
  for.
- **While playing:** When players do something unexpected, relevant people, motives,
  threats, and opportunities may exist in the notes without being readily available in
  the moment.
- **When returning after time away:** The GM may remember individual facts while taking
  longer to recover how the larger world fits together.

The primary problem is not merely buried information. It is that an interconnected world
is represented as isolated pieces.

### Internal problem

The GM feels frustrated by the gap between the rich world they imagined and how much of
that world actually comes alive during play.

> "I know there's more in this world than I'm managing to bring to the table."

They may recognize a useful connection only after a session, overlook a consequence that
could have enriched the story, or feel that the campaign experienced by the players is
thinner than the campaign in their imagination.

Supporting feelings include:

- Frustration when preparation does not pay off.
- Uncertainty about whether the world will hold together.
- Regret when a useful connection becomes apparent only afterward.
- Concern that the campaign at the table feels thinner than the world in their
  imagination.

### Philosophical problem

- **Creating a rich world is hard work. Putting that work to use shouldn't be.**
- A GM's best ideas should not become less useful simply because they were written on
  different pages.

## 3. Guide

### Empathy

- I know the frustration of building a world full of depth, only to realize afterward
  that a character, relationship, or consequence could have made a moment much richer.
- I also know how difficult it can be to understand a character's motives without seeing
  the history, environment, relationships, and forces that shaped them.
- Rich campaigns are not merely collections of notes. They are evolving systems whose
  people, places, motives, and events continually affect one another.

### Authority

- I began building MasterMind while adapting an ambitious campaign for my family. I
  wanted the at-a-glance relationships of a visual map and the depth of detailed campaign
  notes in one place.
- As I mapped the campaign, seeing its elements together helped me notice questions,
  connections, and playable possibilities I had not considered while reading the source
  material or working in separate notes.
- I am both a game master and a product designer with more than 20 years of experience
  designing games, educational products, healthcare tools, and complex data systems.

**MasterMind was built around a practical question:**

> What becomes possible when a GM can think with their entire campaign in front of them?

## 4. Plan

### Process

1. **Map the world**
2. **Develop its possibilities**
3. **Ready it for play**

#### 1. Map the world

Create, arrange, group, and connect the people, places, factions, motives, histories,
events, and other elements that make up the campaign.

Mapping includes both the story elements and their relationships. The map can begin with
the campaign's connected foundations and grow as the world develops.

**Result:** The GM can see the world as an interconnected system rather than a collection
of isolated notes.

#### 2. Develop its possibilities

Use the map to examine how the world fits together:

- Test whether motivations, histories, and relationships make sense.
- Identify missing connective tissue.
- Follow how people, places, factions, and events affect one another.
- Explore tensions, opportunities, risks, and consequences.
- Resolve contradictions.
- Add, revise, and reconnect elements as understanding evolves.

A useful recurring question is:

> **If this is true, what else must be true?**

MasterMind does not answer that question for the GM. It keeps enough of the surrounding
world visible to help the GM recognize where the question may be useful.

**Result:** Richer characters, clearer motivations, more coherent world logic, and
credible possibilities worth developing.

#### 3. Ready it for play

Turn the strongest possibilities into material the GM can use:

- Character intentions and likely reactions.
- Hooks and opportunities.
- Secrets and clues.
- Active threats and faction moves.
- Consequences of player choices.
- Flexible situations.
- Relevant context for improvisation.

The GM is not scripting the players' route. They are preparing a world capable of
responding coherently when the players act.

After play changes the world, the GM updates the map and the cycle continues.

**Result:** More of the GM's creativity becomes available during preparation and play.

## 6. Success

MasterMind helps create the conditions for more of the GM's imagined world to become
available during worldbuilding, preparation, and play.

With a connected visual representation, the GM can:

- Consider characters within their histories, relationships, and surroundings.
- Explore how factions, places, motives, and events affect one another.
- Notice questions and possibilities that are easier to miss across separate notes.
- Follow how a choice or event could produce consequences elsewhere.
- Develop credible possibilities without committing to a predetermined path.
- Access a broader set of relevant story elements when preparing or improvising.
- Return after time away and recover how the world fits together.
- Spend more attention imagining and responding and less attention remembering where to
  look.

This can help create campaign moments in which:

- Character actions emerge from established motives and relationships.
- Earlier clues return with new meaning.
- Player choices produce consequences elsewhere in the world.
- Separate story elements converge into meaningful possibilities.
- New threats reach people and places the players already value.
- More of the GM's preparation becomes available at the table.

MasterMind does not guarantee creative breakthroughs, player investment, emotional
reactions, or a successful campaign. The GM creates and delivers those experiences.
MasterMind helps them develop and use more of the world they have created.

**Controlling success:** More of the world the GM imagined becomes part of the world the
players experience.

## 7. Failure

Without a connected view, the GM may build a rich world while too much of its depth
remains trapped between pages.

Without a connected view:

- Characters may feel less rooted in their histories, relationships, and surroundings
  than intended.
- Missing connections may remain unnoticed.
- Consequences may remain local instead of affecting the wider world.
- Preparation may draw primarily from what comes to mind first.
- Relevant threats, clues, and opportunities may surface only after a session.
- Improvisation may use a narrower portion of the campaign than the GM has already
  created.
- The world experienced by the players may feel thinner than the world in the GM's
  imagination.

**Controlling failure:** Too much of the world remains trapped between pages.

This is unrealized creative potential — not campaign collapse, loss of control, or damage
to the GM's reputation or relationships.

### Extra notes on failure

The GM's notes may contain a rich world, but the players experience only the portion the
GM could find, recall, or connect in the moment.

- Carefully written material remains buried.
- Characters and places can feel more isolated or shallow than intended.
- Consequences and opportunities are easier to overlook and often remain local instead of
  rippling through the world.
- Preparation concentrates on the path the GM predicts and/or expects.
- Improvisation draws on only the material that can be recalled or found quickly.
- Useful threads surfacing only after the session. *"I wish I would have thought of that
  before _______"* — everyone who's ever GM'd a campaign.
- Hours of imaginative work producing less value than they could have.
- The campaign at the table feeling thinner than the campaign in the GM's imagination.

### Research-supported mechanism

- External representations and visible contextual cues can affect which relationships,
  questions, and inferences become available for consideration.
- Flexible GM-preparation frameworks commonly emphasize situations, relationships,
  motivations, clues, and reusable material rather than fixed plots.

### Product hypotheses the beta must validate

- Other GMs notice more useful questions, relationships, and possibilities.
- The process helps them develop richer characters and more coherent consequences.
- A connected view makes a broader portion of the campaign available during preparation.
- Relevant context becomes easier to access during play.
- GMs feel more prepared when players take unexpected directions.
- More of their imaginative work produces value at the table.

### Claims MasterMind should not make

- MasterMind makes everyone more creative.
- The map automatically produces better ideas.
- Every user will reproduce the founder's experience.
- MasterMind literally reveals or evaluates every possible option.
- A connected map guarantees coherent stories or invested players.
- The GM can only combine ideas that are currently visible.

## Appendix A — Evidence base from the 2026-07-22 research pass

> Four research agents fact-checked the claims underneath this script. Full citations
> lived in that session's transcript and were not carried forward verbatim; what follows
> is the claim-level summary. If a specific citation is ever needed (it should never be
> needed for the launch post — a Discord post citing meta-analyses would be insufferable),
> re-run the research pass.
>
> **The honest story is RETRIEVAL/ACCESS, not "sparks creativity."** All five defensible
> claims point the same way.

### Five defensible claims

1. **Recognition beats recall.** People can't *list* what they'd instantly *recognize*
   (~20% free recall vs. 83–92% recognition in the studies reviewed). This is the core
   argument: a binder demands recall; a canvas offers recognition.
2. **Co-location beats scatter.** Things you must connect are easier to use when visible
   together than when scattered (supported by two meta-analyses).
3. **Cueing.** Having knowledge isn't using it — uncued, ~30% of people apply relevant
   knowledge they possess; pointed at it, 90%+.
4. **Improvisation runs on reachable preparation.** Skilled improvisation draws on
   prepared material that can be reached instantly (jazz + theatrical improv literature,
   plus two peer-reviewed TTRPG studies that independently proposed a MasterMind-like
   tool from GM interviews).
5. **Access cost is decisive.** Hard-to-reach information simply doesn't get reached for.

### Banned claims (wrong, myth, or unsupported — do not use)

- "7±2 items in working memory" (folk version is a myth).
- "You forget 70% in a day" (Ebbinghaus misapplied).
- "Recall collapses under pressure."
- "Memory palace" framing.
- The "Google effect."
- **"See your whole campaign at once"** — node-link views *lose* to tables past ~20
  nodes for most tasks, EXCEPT tracing connections. The provable claim is "trace
  connections between anything," not "see everything."
- "More visible = more creative" — the evidence runs the OTHER way (design fixation).
- "23 minutes to refocus after an interruption."
- "Hours lost searching your notes."
- "Group flow" stated as fact.

### A claim we killed

- "You can only combine what you can see" — falsified by the blindfolded-architect
  study. (Already carried in §11's should-not-make list.)

### Two evidence-backed side effects

- **Familiarity substitutes for authorship.** The reason a stranger finds a 100-node
  personal graph overwhelming is that the map works for its author; a widely known story
  removes that barrier. Recorded as an *input* to the launch-demo decision, which is
  **open** (Erik, 2026-07-28 — no call made either way). The other known inputs, from a
  2026-07-28 review: spoiler, IP/copyright, and audience-comprehension concerns around
  using published adventure material (e.g., Curse of Strahd) in a public demo. All
  inputs inform the decision; none settles it. Evaluate when the demonstration is
  actually being designed.
- **Unstructured layouts cause measurable disorientation** — a research-backed argument
  for the lines / shapes / regions organization features.
